A.3: Warp the images

We rectify images via inverse warping using the same homography. The geometric mapping is identical; differences are purely in the resampling method.

Nearest Neighbor fastest

  • Look: crisp but jaggy. Slanted edges (e.g., desk/partition seam and poster edges) show clear stair-stepping and aliasing. Fine textures can appear noisy or “sparkly”.
  • Pros: very fast; preserves discrete labels (useful for masks/segmentations).
  • Cons: visible artifacts on diagonals/thin lines; can produce moiré on repetitive patterns.

Bilinear better quality

  • Look: visually smoother. The same slanted edges become continuous with much less stair-stepping; repetitive textures exhibit far less aliasing. Small text/high-frequency detail is slightly softened (minor blur).
  • Pros: reduces aliasing and edge artifacts; usually preferred for perceptual quality and for blending in mosaics.
  • Cons: mild blur; a bit slower than NN (still cheap in practice).

Conclusions

  • For rectification deliverables, bilinear yields cleaner edges and fewer artifacts; NN is great for quick debugging.
  • For mosaics, bilinear generally produces better seams and fewer high-frequency artifacts, especially when combined with feathering or pyramid blending.
  • Both methods avoid holes thanks to inverse warping; geometry is identical—the difference is only photometric (resampling).

A.4: Blend images into a mosaic

We form a mosaic by warping one image into the coordinate system of the other and combining them with soft alpha feathering (weighted averaging). The geometry comes from the homography; the visual smoothness comes from our weights.

Inputs & Outputs

  • Inputs: im1 (to be warped), im2 (reference), H_1to2 (homography mapping im1 → im2), a warper warp_func (either warpImageNearestNeighbor or warpImageBilinear), and blending parameters feather, falloff, gamma, bg.
  • Output: the blended mosaic image.

Procedure (what blend_pair does)

  1. Warp im1 into im2’s frame (inverse warping). We call warp_func(im1, H_1to2, fill_value=bg) which returns: the warped image w1, a binary alpha a1 (0 outside, 255 where samples are valid), and the integer offset of the warped patch within the mosaic canvas.
  2. Compute the mosaic canvas bounds. We take the union of the reference image extent and the warped patch (using the returned offset and size) to get the mosaic width W and height H.
  3. Initialize accumulators. Two arrays of shape (H, W, 3):
    • canvas_sum: accumulates pixel * weight
    • canvas_wsum: accumulates weight
    These let us do a stable weighted average at the end.
  4. Build soft alpha weights (feathering). For each image we create a smooth weight map:
    • Start with a binary mask (all 255 for im2; the returned a1 for w1).
    • Run a separable box blur with odd kernel size ksize = falloff. We implement the blur with an integral-image style 1-D pass that preserves the original array size.
    • Normalize to [0,1], zero outside original support, and optionally apply gamma to sharpen or widen the ramp.
    The helper is _soft_alpha(alpha_uint8, ksize, gamma).
  5. Accumulate both layers. Paste im2 (unwarped) at its natural location and w1 at its offset: 
    canvas_sum  += patch * weight
canvas_wsum += weight
    We use _accumulate(...) to handle offsets, channels, and broadcasting.
  6. Normalize to get the mosaic. mosaic = canvas_sum / max(canvas_wsum, ε) with a small ε to avoid division by zero.

A.5: Bells and Whistles

Instead of projecting each image onto a plane, we project it onto the surface of a cylinder and then “unroll” the cylinder to a flat image. With focal length \(f\) (in pixels) and principal point \((c_x,c_y)\), the horizontal coordinate is reparameterized by an angle, which reduces perspective distortion for wide fields of view:

\[ x' = f\,\arctan\!\Big(\frac{x-c_x}{f}\Big), \qquad y' = f\,\frac{(y-c_y)}{\sqrt{(x-c_x)^2+f^2}} + c_y. \]

  • Vertical lines remain (nearly) straight. A vertical facade or column maps to a constant \(x'\) (the cylinder is aligned with the vertical axis), so vertical edges mostly stay straight.
  • Horizontal lines become curves. Lines such as the roofline or floor grout run across changing viewing directions; after \(x'=\!f\arctan(\cdot)\), they appear as gentle arcs. Only the horizontal line passing exactly through the optical axis stays nearly straight.
  • Black wedges at the top/bottom. These are regions with no valid samples after unrolling the cylinder; they can be cropped in the final mosaic.
